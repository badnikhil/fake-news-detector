\begin{abstract}
%TODO(ESE): add the measured headline numbers (classifier macro-F1, end-to-end 5-class macro-F1) once trained.
Most automatic ``fake news'' detectors are binary text classifiers: they learn how fabricated articles tend to be written rather than whether the claims in them are true, so they cannot explain a verdict, cannot describe an item that is only partly wrong or true-but-out-of-date, and cannot admit that they do not know. We present a compact, fully open-source verification pipeline that can run on a student laptop. Given a headline, an article or a URL, the system extracts check-worthy claims, retrieves evidence from free online sources and from an offline index of fact-checked statements, labels the stance of every evidence passage with a natural-language-inference cross-encoder, compares publication dates to detect old news recirculated as ``breaking'', and fuses these signals with transparent rules into one of five verdicts---\cls{Real}, \cls{Fake}, \cls{Partially True}, \cls{Misleading} or \cls{Unverifiable}---together with a confidence score, dated evidence cards and a plain-language explanation. A fine-tuned DistilBERT content classifier with LIME token attributions contributes only to the confidence estimate. A cross-dataset audit shows why the in-domain accuracies near 99\,\% reported on standard corpora do not transfer. Code, data splits and the claim set are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% ===========================================================================

News now reaches most people through feeds and messaging groups rather than through editors, and the damage done by what slips through is well documented. Tracing roughly 126{,}000 story cascades on Twitter over eleven years, Vosoughi \emph{et al.}\ found that false stories were about 70\,\% more likely to be retweeted than true ones and that the truth needed roughly six times longer to reach 1{,}500 people~\cite{vosoughi2018spread}. The World Economic Forum's \emph{Global Risks Report 2024} places misinformation and disinformation first among global risks for the coming two years, ahead of extreme weather and armed conflict~\cite{wef2024risks}. A study of the COVID-19 ``infodemic'' linked a single rumour---that drinking concentrated alcohol or methanol cures the virus---to about 800 deaths and roughly 5{,}800 hospital admissions in the first three months of 2020~\cite{islam2020infodemic}. India, the setting of this work, offers its own examples: child-kidnapping rumours forwarded on WhatsApp were linked to at least 17 mob killings across the country between April and July 2018~\cite{bbc2018whatsapp,aljazeera2018whatsapp}, and Indian fact-checkers routinely find genuine footage of earlier floods, riots and accidents re-captioned as ``breaking'' news for a new event---in one analysis of 125 fact-checked Indian COVID-19 items, nearly half paired text with video and 94\,\% originated on social platforms~\cite{alzaman2021india}. This last pattern---true material presented in the wrong time or place---is one that most detectors are not built to see.

Manual fact-checking is accurate but slow. Most academic work on automatic detection has therefore taken a shortcut: treat the task as binary text classification and let a model learn the surface cues of fabricated writing. Such classifiers report accuracies above 95\,\% on benchmark corpora~\cite{verma2021welfake,kaliyar2021fakebert}, but the numbers hide three weaknesses. First, they learn \emph{style} and dataset artefacts rather than \emph{facts}: a corpus whose genuine articles all come from one wire service can be separated almost perfectly by boilerplate alone~\cite{ahmed2017isot}, and the same models lose much of their accuracy on a different corpus. Second, a binary label cannot express the shapes real misinformation takes---a true event with a wrong number, a real quotation attached to the wrong occasion, a 2018 video captioned as today's news. Third, a classifier always produces an answer; it cannot say that the evidence is insufficient, which for hyper-local or very recent claims is the only honest response.

\textbf{Problem statement.} Given a news item---a headline, an article text or a URL---automatically determine, with supporting evidence, dates and a human-readable explanation, whether its central claims are real, fake, partially true, misleading or unverifiable, using only free tools and hardware available to students.

We address this problem by moving the decision from the classifier to the evidence: the system selects the check-worthy sentences of an item, retrieves passages from free web search, Wikipedia, the Google Fact Check Tools API and an offline index of fact-checked statements, labels each passage as supporting, refuting or neutral, compares the dates involved, and applies a small, inspectable set of rules. A style-based classifier is retained, but it may only adjust the confidence or break a single-source tie; it never outputs \cls{Fake} or \cls{Real} on its own when evidence exists. The contributions of this paper are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An evidence-grounded five-class verdict scheme with abstention.} We define \cls{Real}, \cls{Fake}, \cls{Partially True}, \cls{Misleading} and \cls{Unverifiable} operationally, map LIAR's six PolitiFact grades onto them, and reach each verdict through a published decision table whose confidence reflects evidence agreement, coverage and source reliability, so every output carries the rule that fired, the evidence used and an explicit uncertainty statement (objective O6).
  \item \textbf{A laptop-scale claim-to-verdict pipeline.} Claim identification, evidence retrieval from free sources with an offline FAISS fallback, NLI stance detection with a small cross-encoder, and a temporal/context check that flags old news presented as new and entity--date mismatches, all running on CPU within seconds offline (objectives O3--O5).
  \item \textbf{An explainable content classifier evaluated honestly.} A fine-tuned DistilBERT model with LIME token attributions serves as the style signal; we report in-domain results on WELFake, a cross-dataset transfer and leakage audit against ISOT, and an ablation of how much the classifier adds to the evidence-based verdict (objective O1).
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys related work and states the gap; Sections~\ref{sec:data}--\ref{sec:setup} describe the datasets, the pipeline with its fusion rules, and the experimental setup; Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} compares them with existing systems, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
%% ===========================================================================

We group prior work into five themes that correspond to the components of our system; Table~\ref{tab:lit} summarises the most relevant papers along the dimensions that matter to us.

\subsection{Datasets and Benchmarks}

LIAR~\cite{wang2017liar} collects 12.8k short political statements rated by PolitiFact on a six-point scale from \emph{pants-on-fire} to \emph{true}. Its best text-and-metadata model reached only about 27\,\% six-way accuracy, which remains a useful reminder that fine-grained truthfulness cannot be read off the wording of a sentence. ISOT~\cite{ahmed2017isot,ahmed2018opinion} pairs 21k Reuters articles with 23k articles from sites flagged by fact-checkers; because every genuine article shares one source, the corpus is easy to separate and is widely suspected of source leakage. WELFake~\cite{verma2021welfake} merges four public corpora into 72k articles to reduce exactly this kind of overfitting and is our primary training set; one of its four sources is ISOT itself, and our preprocessing audit found that almost every ISOT article recurs verbatim in WELFake (Section~\ref{sec:data}), which is why our cross-corpus experiment removes the shared articles from the WELFake side. FakeNewsNet~\cite{shu2020fakenewsnet} adds social context and PolitiFact/GossipCop labels to news items; we use its PolitiFact titles in our offline index. On the verification side, FEVER~\cite{thorne2018fever} contributes 185k claims written against Wikipedia and labelled \emph{Supports}, \emph{Refutes} or \emph{Not Enough Info}, defining the retrieve-then-verify task that our stance module inherits, while MultiFC~\cite{augenstein2019multifc} gathers 35k claims with the heterogeneous verdict labels of 26 real fact-checking sites. ClaimBuster~\cite{arslan2020claimbuster} provides 23.5k debate sentences annotated for check-worthiness. Surveys by Zhou and Zafarani~\cite{zhou2020survey} and Guo \emph{et al.}~\cite{guo2022survey} organise this landscape: the former distinguishes knowledge-, style-, propagation- and source-based detection, and the latter formalises fact-checking as claim detection, evidence retrieval, verdict prediction and justification production---the sequence our pipeline follows.

\subsection{Content-Based Classifiers}

The earliest and still most common approach treats detection as document classification. Ahmed \emph{et al.}~\cite{ahmed2017isot} reported that TF-IDF n-grams with a linear SVM reach roughly 92\,\% accuracy on ISOT, and P{\'e}rez-Rosas \emph{et al.}~\cite{perezrosas2018automatic} found linear models on lexical, syntactic and readability features to be competitive on crowd-sourced fake news. Pre-trained transformers~\cite{devlin2019bert} raised these numbers further: FakeBERT~\cite{kaliyar2021fakebert} feeds BERT embeddings through parallel convolutional blocks and reports 98.9\,\% accuracy on a Kaggle corpus, and WELFake's own linguistic-feature model reports 96.7\,\%. DistilBERT~\cite{sanh2019distilbert} keeps about 97\,\% of BERT's language-understanding performance at 40\,\% fewer parameters, which is what makes fine-tuning on a 4\,GB consumer GPU feasible and is why we adopt it. What these papers share is an in-domain, binary evaluation; none reports cross-corpus transfer, and none can tell a user \emph{why} an article is suspicious beyond its wording. We keep such a classifier, but only as a secondary signal.

\subsection{Claim Detection, Evidence Retrieval and Fact Verification}

ClaimBuster~\cite{hassan2017claimbuster} showed that check-worthy sentences in political debates can be ranked with sentence-level features and a supervised classifier, and its later benchmark release~\cite{arslan2020claimbuster} is the standard training set for the task; our claim identifier combines similar hand-crafted cues with an optional classifier trained on that data. For verification, the FEVER baseline~\cite{thorne2018fever} chains document retrieval, sentence selection and an NLI model and scored 31.9 FEVER points; the shared-task winner, NSMN~\cite{nie2019nsmn}, unified the three steps with semantic matching networks, and KGAT~\cite{liu2020kgat} propagates evidence through a kernel-based graph attention network to exceed 70 FEVER points---the current ceiling for Wikipedia-grounded verification. DeClarE~\cite{popat2018declare} verifies free-text claims against web search results with attention over the retrieved articles and a source-credibility embedding, which is close in spirit to our online path. Two components make retrieval and stance practical at our scale: Sentence-BERT~\cite{reimers2019sbert} bi-encoders (we use the 22M-parameter MiniLM variant~\cite{wang2020minilm}) turn semantic search over thousands of fact-checked statements into a millisecond nearest-neighbour lookup, while a small DeBERTaV3 cross-encoder~\cite{he2023debertav3} trained on SNLI and MultiNLI~\cite{williams2018mnli} provides an off-the-shelf three-way stance judgement on CPU. Hanselowski \emph{et al.}~\cite{hanselowski2018stance} re-examined the Fake News Challenge and showed that headline-body stance systems that looked strong under the challenge metric had far lower macro-F1 on the minority \emph{agree}/\emph{disagree} classes---a caution we take into account by reporting macro-F1 throughout.

\subsection{Explainability}

LIME~\cite{ribeiro2016lime} explains an individual prediction by fitting a sparse linear surrogate on perturbed inputs, and SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017shap} unifies several attribution methods under Shapley values; both are model-agnostic and produce token-level highlights that lay users can read. In the fake-news setting, dEFEND~\cite{shu2019defend} co-attends over article sentences and user comments so that the sentences and comments that drove the decision can be shown as the explanation, and DeClarE~\cite{popat2018declare} exposes the retrieved evidence words its attention focused on. These methods explain a \emph{model}; they do not by themselves ground the verdict in checkable facts. We therefore treat LIME highlights as an explanation of the style signal only, and build the primary explanation from the retrieved evidence, its stance, its source and its date.

\subsection{Temporal and Contextual Misinformation}

Much misinformation is not fabricated text but genuine material displaced in time or place. Vosoughi \emph{et al.}~\cite{vosoughi2018spread} quantified how much faster and deeper falsehoods travel, and Zubiaga \emph{et al.}~\cite{zubiaga2018rumours} survey rumour detection, tracking and stance classification as an inherently temporal process in which a story's veracity is resolved over time. In India, Al-Zaman~\cite{alzaman2021india} analysed 125 fact-checked COVID-19 stories and found that two-thirds concerned health, that text paired with video was the commonest format, and that almost all originated on social platforms rather than in mainstream media; reporting on the 2018 WhatsApp lynchings~\cite{bbc2018whatsapp,aljazeera2018whatsapp} traced the killings to rumour messages and videos forwarded as local warnings. Existing detectors ignore publication dates entirely; we add a lightweight check that compares the input's date and recency cues against the dates of the supporting evidence and against the entities it mentions.

\subsection{The Gap}

Table~\ref{tab:lit} makes the gap visible. Style-based classifiers are accurate in-domain but produce no evidence, no partial-truth labels and no abstention; FEVER-style verifiers ground their decisions in evidence but assume Wikipedia-only, synthetic claims and do not consider time; explainability work explains models rather than facts. No system we are aware of combines (i) a five-way verdict with an explicit \cls{Unverifiable} class, (ii) evidence retrieval from free sources with an offline fallback, (iii) a temporal and contextual check, and (iv) evidence-linked explanations, while running on ordinary student hardware. Those properties are the three contributions listed in Section~I, and the rest of this paper builds and evaluates them.

Table~\ref{tab:data} lists the corpora and the role each plays. \textbf{WELFake}~\cite{verma2021welfake} (72{,}134 articles) is the primary training set for the content classifier and \textbf{ISOT}~\cite{ahmed2017isot} (44{,}898 articles) the secondary one. \textbf{LIAR}~\cite{wang2017liar} (12{,}836 PolitiFact statements, official splits) trains a three-way truthfulness head and, together with 1{,}056 \textbf{FakeNewsNet}~\cite{shu2020fakenewsnet} PolitiFact titles, populates the offline fact-check index. A balanced \textbf{FEVER}~\cite{thorne2018fever} subset (20{,}000 train + 3{,}000 dev claims) evaluates the stance model. LIAR's six grades map onto our scheme as true/mostly-true $\rightarrow$ \cls{Real}, half-true $\rightarrow$ \cls{Partially True}, barely-true $\rightarrow$ \cls{Misleading} and false/pants-fire $\rightarrow$ \cls{Fake}; the three-way collapse used for the LIAR head is TRUE / MIXED / FALSE. \cls{Unverifiable} has no counterpart in any corpus---it arises only from retrieval failure at run time.

\textbf{Live Claims Set.} For end-to-end evaluation we built 95 recent claims (fact-checks published between February~2025 and 25~October~2026, 93 of them in 2026) from PolitiFact (46), Snopes (31), Lead Stories (9), BOOM (4), Alt News (3) and Full Fact (2). Each rating, claim text and date was read from the live fact-check page and mapped to the five classes (e.g.\ Snopes \emph{Miscaptioned}/\emph{Outdated} $\rightarrow$ \cls{Misleading}); \cls{Unverifiable} comes only from Lead Stories evidence-absence ratings. Class counts are \cls{Fake} 27, \cls{Real} 21, \cls{Partially True} 19, \cls{Misleading} 19, \cls{Unverifiable} 9. A stratified 30-item dev slice (seed 42) is reserved for tuning fusion thresholds; the remaining 65 items are held out.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} All corpora are converted to one schema (\texttt{id, title, text, label, \dots, split}). For WELFake and ISOT we (i) strip publisher and format boilerplate with 26 named text patterns and 2 title patterns---Reuters datelines, photo credits, captions, bylines, \texttt{[VIDEO]} tags, outlet suffixes, URL debris---while keeping content words such as ``told Reuters''; (ii) drop empty and very short texts; (iii) de-duplicate greedily, first exact SHA-1 matches and then MinHash near-duplicates (Jaccard $\geq 0.9$); and (iv) split 80/10/10, stratified, seed 42, giving 48{,}639 / 6{,}080 / 6{,}080 WELFake and 30{,}053 / 3{,}757 / 3{,}757 ISOT rows. Splits are stored as id lists and every model reads the same ids.

\textbf{Leakage audit: ISOT $\subset$ WELFake.} WELFake was assembled from four corpora, one of which is ISOT. Hashing normalised text shows that 37{,}542 of the 37{,}567 processed ISOT articles (99.93\,\%) occur verbatim in processed WELFake, so ``train on WELFake, test on ISOT'' is an in-domain experiment. For the cross-domain experiment we therefore use \textbf{WELFake$\setminus$ISOT}: the WELFake rows not hash-matched to ISOT, minus 25 MinHash near-duplicates, i.e.\ 23{,}232 rows. A second audit trains a logistic regression on the 26 artefact-pattern counts alone (no words): it reaches 0.9973 five-fold accuracy on raw ISOT but 0.5568 on processed ISOT, which confirms that raw ISOT can be separated by source boilerplate alone and that our stripping removes that shortcut.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
%% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Claim Identification}
The input is cleaned and sentence-split with spaCy \texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm}. Sentences of fewer than six tokens, questions and imperatives are dropped; each remaining sentence $s$ at position $i$ receives the check-worthiness score
\begin{multline}
h(s) = 1.0\,[\mathrm{NE}] + 0.8\,[\mathrm{NUM}] + 0.6\,[\mathrm{REP}] + 0.5\,[i<3] \\
 + 0.4\,[\mathrm{CMP}] - 0.8\,[\mathrm{OPN}] - 0.5\,[\mathrm{HDG}],
\end{multline}
where the indicators denote a PERSON/ORG/GPE/EVENT/NORP/LOC entity, a CARDINAL/PERCENT/MONEY/DATE/QUANTITY entity, a reporting verb (\emph{said, announced, according to}, \dots), a lead sentence, a number or comparative, a first-person opinion cue (\emph{I think, should, must}) and a hedge (\emph{may, might, could}). The score is normalised to $[0,1]$ by its maximum (3.3) and clipped. Near-duplicate sentences (MiniLM cosine $>0.85$) are removed and the top $k=5$ are kept; a headline is always claim~1 with score 1. The optional ClaimBuster-trained classifier~\cite{arslan2020claimbuster} is not used yet.

\subsection{Evidence Retrieval}
\emph{Online}, each claim is sent to DuckDuckGo text search (\texttt{ddgs}), the Wikipedia search API (top page intros) and, when an API key is configured, the Google Fact Check Tools \texttt{claims:search} endpoint, each with a 6\,s timeout. Result pages are fetched and extracted with trafilatura~\cite{barbaresi2021trafilatura}, dated with htmldate, and reduced to the three sentences most similar to the claim. All network answers are cached in SQLite so that runs are repeatable. \emph{Offline}, the claim is embedded with \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}~\cite{reimers2019sbert,wang2020minilm} and searched in a FAISS~\cite{johnson2021faiss} \texttt{IndexFlatIP} over L2-normalised vectors (cosine) of all 12{,}836 LIAR statements and 1{,}056 FakeNewsNet titles (13{,}892 vectors, 20.35\,MB); hits with cosine $<0.55$ are discarded. Each item receives a source-reliability weight $w_s$ from an editable domain list: fact-checkers 1.0, wire services and official sites 0.8, Wikipedia 0.6, unknown domains 0.3, satire/hoax sites 0 (flagged).

\subsection{Stance Detection}
Each (claim, evidence) pair is scored by the cross-encoder \texttt{cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-small}, a DeBERTaV3~\cite{he2023debertav3} model trained on SNLI~\cite{bowman2015snli} and MultiNLI~\cite{williams2018mnli} only; we do not fine-tune it. With premise = evidence and hypothesis = claim, entailment, contradiction and neutral map to \textsc{Supports}, \textsc{Refutes} and \textsc{Neutral} with probability $p$. For items that carry a fact-checker rating (LIAR, FakeNewsNet, Google Fact Check) the rating is turned into a stance with $p=0.95$, but only when the claim restates the rated statement (NLI entailment, or cosine $\geq 0.75$ without contradiction). The signed contribution of an item is $\sigma(e)=\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{stance})\cdot p\cdot w_s$.

\subsection{Temporal and Context Check}
Three flags are computed for the main claim. \emph{Old news}: the input contains a recency cue (\emph{breaking, just in, today, \dots}) and the gap between the input date $t_\mathrm{post}$ and the (lower) median date of the supporting evidence is at least $N=6$ months. \emph{Out of context}: the claim's GPE/DATE/EVENT entities conflict with those of at least two supporting passages. \emph{Headline--body mismatch}: NLI with the body as premise refutes the headline with $p\geq 0.7$.

\subsection{Verdict Fusion and Confidence}
For claim $i$ let $S_i=\sum_e \sigma(e)$ and let $E_i$ be the number of non-neutral items with $w_s\geq 0.5$. The main stance is \textsc{Supports} if $S_1\geq\tau$, \textsc{Refutes} if $S_1\leq-\tau$ and \textsc{Neutral} otherwise, with $\tau=0.6$. Table~\ref{tab:rules} lists the decision rules, evaluated top to bottom (first match wins); the thresholds are those of \texttt{src/fusion/fusion.yaml} ($\tau=0.6$, $N=6$, $k=5$, minimum evidence 2), which are defaults not yet tuned on the dev slice. The content classifier's fake probability $p_\mathrm{fake}$ appears in only two places: the R8 tie-break and a $\pm 0.10$ confidence adjustment.

\label{tab:rules}
\centering\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l p{5.3cm} l@{}}

The confidence combines how one-sided, how broad and how reliable the evidence for the main claim is:
\begin{align}
a &= |S_1| \,/\, \textstyle\left(\sum_e p_e w_e + 10^{-6}\right), \quad c = \min(E_1,4)/4,\\
q &= \mathrm{mean}(w_s \text{ over non-neutral items}),\\
\mathrm{conf} &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(0.5a + 0.3c + 0.2q + \delta,\; 0.05,\; 0.98\right),
\end{align}
where $\delta=+0.10$ if the classifier agrees with a \cls{Fake}/\cls{Real} verdict ($p_\mathrm{fake}\geq 0.7$ / $\leq 0.3$), $-0.10$ if it disagrees and 0 otherwise. For \cls{Unverifiable}, $\mathrm{conf}=1-\max(a,0.2)$, i.e.\ how sure the system is that it cannot tell. R0 and the language check use fixed confidences of 0.9 and 0.8. Every response returns the rule that fired, the claims, the evidence cards with source, date, cosine and stance, and the temporal flags.

\subsection{Content Classifiers}
We train TF-IDF word $n$-gram models with multinomial Naive Bayes, logistic regression and a linear SVM~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}, and fine-tune \texttt{distilbert-base-uncased}~\cite{sanh2019distilbert,wolf2020transformers} on \texttt{title [SEP] text}. A second DistilBERT head is fine-tuned on LIAR statements (text only, no speaker or party, which are label proxies) for TRUE / MIXED / FALSE with class-balanced cross-entropy. Until a full-data DistilBERT checkpoint exists, the deployed style signal is the TF-IDF + logistic-regression model.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
%% ===========================================================================

\textbf{Splits and seed.} WELFake and ISOT use the stratified 80/10/10 split (seed 42); LIAR uses its official 10{,}269 / 1{,}284 / 1{,}283 split; the FEVER subset uses 3{,}000 balanced dev claims. Model selection always uses validation macro-F1; the test split is used once per model.

\textbf{Classical models.} \texttt{GridSearchCV} with stratified 5-fold CV on the WELFake training split (48{,}639 rows), scoring macro-F1, over $n$-gram range $\{(1,1),(1,2)\}$ $\times$ max.\ features $\{50\mathrm{k},100\mathrm{k},200\mathrm{k}\}$ $\times$ sublinear TF $\{\mathrm{T},\mathrm{F}\}$ $\times$ $C\in\{0.1,1,10\}$ (LR, SVM; 36 configurations each) or $\alpha\in\{0.1,0.5,1.0\}$ (NB; 18). Winners (best CV macro-F1): NB $\alpha=0.1$, 200k features (0.8684); LR $C=10$, 100k features (0.9428); SVM $C=1$, 200k features (0.9459); all with $(1,2)$-grams and sublinear TF. The winners are refitted on the full training split of each dataset.

\textbf{DistilBERT.} Planned grid: learning rate $\{2,3,5\}\times10^{-5}$ $\times$ max.\ length $\{256,512\}$ on a 20{,}000-row stratified subset of WELFake train, 3 epochs with the best epoch kept, AdamW with linear schedule, warmup ratio 0.1, weight decay 0.01, fp16, batch 16 at length 256 (8 $\times$ 2 gradient accumulation at 512), evaluated on the full validation split (6{,}080). The three length-256 runs were completed; the length-512 runs and the full-data retrain of the winner were stopped on a team decision and are deferred to the final evaluation. LIAR head: learning rate $\{2,3\}\times10^{-5}$ $\times$ epochs $\{3,5\}$, max.\ length 64, batch 16, same optimiser settings.

\textbf{Hardware and software.} One laptop with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2050 (4\,GB), 8 CPU threads and 13\,GB RAM; the stance evaluation and the API run on CPU. Python 3.11, PyTorch 2.14.1, transformers 4.57.6, scikit-learn 1.9.1, sentence-transformers 5.7.0, faiss-cpu 1.15.1, spaCy 3.8.16, FastAPI 0.162.0. \textbf{Metrics:} accuracy, macro precision/recall/F1 and ROC-AUC (SVM from decision margins).

\textbf{Error analysis.} The SVM misclassifies 310 of the 6{,}080 WELFake test articles (164 missed fakes, 146 false alarms). Assigned by fixed, ordered rules, 154 are style overlap (wire-style fakes or tabloid-style real articles), 71 opinion or first-person commentary, 68 cases where a source cue (e.g.\ ``Reuters'', ``via'') points the wrong way, 7 satire, 5 short texts, 4 republished wire copy on fake-labelled sites and 1 near-duplicate with a conflicting label---again errors of style, not of fact.

\subsection{Stance Detection on FEVER}
On 3{,}000 balanced FEVER dev pairs (gold evidence text from the FEVER 2017 Wikipedia dump; NEI claims paired with the sentences retrieved for them by the Papelo system~\cite{malon2018papelo}; premise prefixed with the page title) the cross-encoder reaches \textbf{0.7267 accuracy} and 0.7121 macro-F1 (Table~\ref{tab:stance}, Fig.~\ref{fig:cmstance}). This is a \emph{zero-shot} result---the model was trained on SNLI and MultiNLI only, not on FEVER---and it \textbf{misses our objective O4} ($\geq 0.85$). The shortfall is concentrated in NEI: on the gold \textsc{Supports}/\textsc{Refutes} pairs accuracy is 0.8790, but 479 of 1{,}000 NEI pairs are predicted \textsc{Refutes}, because the retrieved sentences concern the same entity and state a different fact. Without the page-title prefix accuracy falls to 0.6337. In the pipeline this error mode is damped by R3, which needs two reliable refuting sources; a \textsc{Refutes} threshold tuned on FEVER training pairs and an optional one-epoch fine-tune are planned.
