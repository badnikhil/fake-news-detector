\begin{abstract}
Most ``fake news'' detectors are binary text classifiers that learn how fabricated articles are written, not whether their claims are true; they cannot explain a verdict, describe partly wrong or outdated items, or abstain. We present an open-source pipeline that runs on a student laptop. It extracts check-worthy claims, retrieves evidence from free web sources and an offline index of fact-checked statements, labels the stance of every passage with an NLI cross-encoder, checks dates for old news presented as new, and fuses these signals with transparent rules into one of five verdicts---\cls{Real}, \cls{Fake}, \cls{Partially True}, \cls{Misleading} or \cls{Unverifiable}---with a confidence score, dated evidence and an explanation. A fine-tuned DistilBERT style classifier reaches 0.9898 test macro-F1 on WELFake but may only adjust the confidence, because TF-IDF classifiers lose 0.29--0.44 macro-F1 when moved to a disjoint corpus. On 65 held-out fact-checked claims from 2025--26 the full system reaches a five-class macro-F1 of 0.1667 online and 0.0343 offline, far below our 0.55 target, mainly because it abstains too often. We also show that an earlier run leaked each claim's own fact-check page into the evidence, which inflated macro-F1 from 0.1260 to 0.1854. Code, data splits and claim sets are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% ===========================================================================

News now reaches most people through feeds and messaging groups rather than through editors, and the damage done by what slips through is well documented. Tracing roughly 126{,}000 story cascades on Twitter over eleven years, Vosoughi \emph{et al.}\ found that false stories were about 70\,\% more likely to be retweeted than true ones and that the truth needed roughly six times longer to reach 1{,}500 people~\cite{vosoughi2018spread}. India, the setting of this work, offers its own examples: Indian fact-checkers routinely find genuine footage of earlier floods, riots and accidents re-captioned as ``breaking'' news for a new event---in one analysis of 125 fact-checked Indian COVID-19 items, nearly half paired text with video and 94\,\% originated on social platforms~\cite{alzaman2021india}. This last pattern---true material presented in the wrong time or place---is one that most detectors are not built to see.

Manual fact-checking is accurate but slow. Most academic work on automatic detection has therefore taken a shortcut: treat the task as binary text classification and let a model learn the surface cues of fabricated writing. Such classifiers report accuracies above 95\,\% on benchmark corpora~\cite{verma2021welfake,kaliyar2021fakebert}, but the numbers hide three weaknesses. First, they learn \emph{style} and dataset artefacts rather than \emph{facts}: a corpus whose genuine articles all come from one wire service can be separated almost perfectly by boilerplate alone~\cite{ahmed2017isot}, and the same models lose much of their accuracy on a different corpus. Second, a binary label cannot express the shapes real misinformation takes---a true event with a wrong number, a real quotation attached to the wrong occasion, a 2018 video captioned as today's news. Third, a classifier always produces an answer; it cannot say that the evidence is insufficient, which for hyper-local or very recent claims is the only honest response.

\textbf{Problem statement.} Given a news item---a headline, an article text or a URL---automatically determine, with supporting evidence, dates and a human-readable explanation, whether its central claims are real, fake, partially true, misleading or unverifiable, using only free tools and hardware available to students.

We address this problem by moving the decision from the classifier to the evidence: the system selects the check-worthy sentences of an item, retrieves passages from free web search, Wikipedia, the Google Fact Check Tools API and an offline index of fact-checked statements, labels each passage as supporting, refuting or neutral, compares the dates involved, and applies a small, inspectable set of rules. A style-based classifier is retained, but it may only adjust the confidence or break a single-source tie; it never outputs \cls{Fake} or \cls{Real} on its own when evidence exists. The contributions of this paper are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An evidence-grounded five-class verdict scheme with abstention.} We define \cls{Real}, \cls{Fake}, \cls{Partially True}, \cls{Misleading} and \cls{Unverifiable} operationally, map LIAR's six PolitiFact grades onto them, and reach each verdict through a published decision table whose confidence reflects evidence agreement, coverage and source reliability, so every output carries the rule that fired, the evidence used and an explicit uncertainty statement (objective O6).
  \item \textbf{A laptop-scale claim-to-verdict pipeline.} Claim identification, evidence retrieval from free sources with an offline FAISS fallback, NLI stance detection with a small cross-encoder, and a temporal/context check that flags old news presented as new and entity--date mismatches, all running on CPU within seconds offline (objectives O3--O5).
  \item \textbf{An explainable content classifier evaluated honestly.} A fine-tuned DistilBERT model with LIME token attributions serves as the style signal; we report in-domain results on WELFake, a cross-dataset transfer and leakage audit against ISOT, and an ablation of how much the classifier adds to the evidence-based verdict (objective O1).
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys related work and states the gap; Sections~\ref{sec:data} and~\ref{sec:method} describe the datasets and the pipeline with its fusion rules; Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} compares the system with existing ones and lists its limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
%% ===========================================================================

We group prior work into five themes that correspond to the components of our system; Table~\ref{tab:lit} summarises the most relevant papers along the dimensions that matter to us.

\subsection{Datasets and Benchmarks}

LIAR~\cite{wang2017liar} collects 12.8k short political statements rated by PolitiFact on a six-point scale from \emph{pants-on-fire} to \emph{true}. Its best text-and-metadata model reached only about 27\,\% six-way accuracy, which remains a useful reminder that fine-grained truthfulness cannot be read off the wording of a sentence. ISOT~\cite{ahmed2017isot} pairs 21k Reuters articles with 23k articles from sites flagged by fact-checkers; because every genuine article shares one source, the corpus is easy to separate and is widely suspected of source leakage. WELFake~\cite{verma2021welfake} merges four public corpora into 72k articles to reduce exactly this kind of overfitting and is our primary training set; one of its four sources is ISOT itself, and our preprocessing audit found that almost every ISOT article recurs verbatim in WELFake (Section~\ref{sec:data}), which is why our cross-corpus experiment removes the shared articles from the WELFake side. FakeNewsNet~\cite{shu2020fakenewsnet} adds social context and PolitiFact/GossipCop labels to news items; we use its PolitiFact titles in our offline index. On the verification side, FEVER~\cite{thorne2018fever} contributes 185k claims written against Wikipedia and labelled \emph{Supports}, \emph{Refutes} or \emph{Not Enough Info}, defining the retrieve-then-verify task that our stance module inherits. ClaimBuster~\cite{arslan2020claimbuster} provides 23.5k debate sentences annotated for check-worthiness. Surveys by Zhou and Zafarani~\cite{zhou2020survey} and Guo \emph{et al.}~\cite{guo2022survey} organise this landscape: the former distinguishes knowledge-, style-, propagation- and source-based detection, and the latter formalises fact-checking as claim detection, evidence retrieval, verdict prediction and justification production---the sequence our pipeline follows.

\subsection{Content-Based Classifiers}

The earliest and still most common approach treats detection as document classification. Ahmed \emph{et al.}~\cite{ahmed2017isot} reported that TF-IDF n-grams with a linear SVM reach roughly 92\,\% accuracy on ISOT. Pre-trained transformers~\cite{devlin2019bert} raised these numbers further: FakeBERT~\cite{kaliyar2021fakebert} feeds BERT embeddings through parallel convolutional blocks and reports 98.9\,\% accuracy on a Kaggle corpus, and WELFake's own linguistic-feature model reports 96.7\,\%. DistilBERT~\cite{sanh2019distilbert} keeps about 97\,\% of BERT's language-understanding performance at 40\,\% fewer parameters, which is what makes fine-tuning on a 4\,GB consumer GPU feasible and is why we adopt it. Blackledge and Atapour-Abarghouei~\cite{blackledge2021transforming} are a rare exception to in-domain-only evaluation: DistilBERT, BERT and DeBERTa reach 0.989--0.997 accuracy on an ISOT hold-out but only 0.670--0.697 when the ISOT-trained models are tested on another corpus. Apart from that study, what these papers share is an in-domain, binary evaluation without cross-corpus transfer, and none can tell a user \emph{why} an article is suspicious beyond its wording. We keep such a classifier, but only as a secondary signal.

\subsection{Claim Detection, Evidence Retrieval and Fact Verification}

The ClaimBuster benchmark~\cite{arslan2020claimbuster} is the standard training set for check-worthiness detection; our claim identifier combines similar hand-crafted cues with an optional classifier trained on that data. For verification, the FEVER baseline~\cite{thorne2018fever} chains document retrieval, sentence selection and an NLI model and scored 31.9 FEVER points, and KGAT~\cite{liu2020kgat} propagates evidence through a kernel-based graph attention network to exceed 70 FEVER points---the current ceiling for Wikipedia-grounded verification. DeClarE~\cite{popat2018declare} verifies free-text claims against web search results with attention over the retrieved articles and a source-credibility embedding, which is close in spirit to our online path. Two components make retrieval and stance practical at our scale: Sentence-BERT~\cite{reimers2019sbert} bi-encoders (we use the 22M-parameter MiniLM variant~\cite{wang2020minilm}) turn semantic search over thousands of fact-checked statements into a millisecond nearest-neighbour lookup, while a small DeBERTaV3 cross-encoder~\cite{he2023debertav3} trained on SNLI and MultiNLI~\cite{williams2018mnli} provides an off-the-shelf three-way stance judgement on CPU.

\subsection{Explainability}

LIME~\cite{ribeiro2016lime} explains an individual prediction by fitting a sparse linear surrogate on perturbed inputs; it is model-agnostic and produces token-level highlights that lay users can read. In the fake-news setting, dEFEND~\cite{shu2019defend} co-attends over article sentences and user comments so that the sentences and comments that drove the decision can be shown as the explanation, and DeClarE~\cite{popat2018declare} exposes the retrieved evidence words its attention focused on. These methods explain a \emph{model}; they do not by themselves ground the verdict in checkable facts. We therefore treat LIME highlights as an explanation of the style signal only, and build the primary explanation from the retrieved evidence, its stance, its source and its date.

\subsection{Temporal and Contextual Misinformation}

Much misinformation is not fabricated text but genuine material displaced in time or place. Vosoughi \emph{et al.}~\cite{vosoughi2018spread} quantified how much faster and deeper falsehoods travel. In India, Al-Zaman~\cite{alzaman2021india} analysed 125 fact-checked COVID-19 stories and found that two-thirds concerned health, that text paired with video was the commonest format, and that almost all originated on social platforms rather than in mainstream media. Existing detectors ignore publication dates entirely; we add a lightweight check that compares the input's date and recency cues against the dates of the supporting evidence and against the entities it mentions.

\subsection{The Gap}

Table~\ref{tab:lit} makes the gap visible. Style-based classifiers are accurate in-domain but produce no evidence, no partial-truth labels and no abstention; FEVER-style verifiers ground their decisions in evidence but assume Wikipedia-only, synthetic claims and do not consider time; explainability work explains models rather than facts. No system we are aware of combines (i) a five-way verdict with an explicit \cls{Unverifiable} class, (ii) evidence retrieval from free sources with an offline fallback, (iii) a temporal and contextual check, and (iv) evidence-linked explanations, while running on ordinary student hardware. Those properties are the three contributions listed in Section~I, and the rest of this paper builds and evaluates them.

Table~\ref{tab:data} lists the corpora and the role each plays. \textbf{WELFake}~\cite{verma2021welfake} (72{,}134 articles) is the primary training set for the content classifier and \textbf{ISOT}~\cite{ahmed2017isot} (44{,}898 articles) the secondary one. \textbf{LIAR}~\cite{wang2017liar} (12{,}836 PolitiFact statements, official splits) trains a three-way truthfulness head and, together with 1{,}056 \textbf{FakeNewsNet}~\cite{shu2020fakenewsnet} PolitiFact titles, populates the offline fact-check index. A balanced \textbf{FEVER}~\cite{thorne2018fever} subset (20{,}000 train + 3{,}000 dev claims) evaluates the stance model. LIAR's six grades map onto our scheme as true/mostly-true $\rightarrow$ \cls{Real}, half-true $\rightarrow$ \cls{Partially True}, barely-true $\rightarrow$ \cls{Misleading} and false/pants-fire $\rightarrow$ \cls{Fake}; the three-way collapse used for the LIAR head is TRUE / MIXED / FALSE. \cls{Unverifiable} has no counterpart in any corpus---it arises only from retrieval failure at run time.

\textbf{Live Claims Set.} For end-to-end evaluation we built 95 recent claims (fact-checks published between February~2025 and 25~October~2026, 93 of them in 2026) from PolitiFact (46), Snopes (31), Lead Stories (9), BOOM (4), Alt News (3) and Full Fact (2). Each rating, claim text and date was read from the live fact-check page and mapped to the five classes (e.g.\ Snopes \emph{Miscaptioned}/\emph{Outdated} $\rightarrow$ \cls{Misleading}); \cls{Unverifiable} comes only from Lead Stories evidence-absence ratings. Class counts are \cls{Fake} 27, \cls{Real} 21, \cls{Partially True} 19, \cls{Misleading} 19, \cls{Unverifiable} 9. A stratified 30-item dev slice (seed 42) is reserved for tuning fusion thresholds; the remaining 65 items are held out.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} All corpora are converted to one schema (\texttt{id, title, text, label, \dots, split}). For WELFake and ISOT we (i) strip publisher and format boilerplate with 26 named text patterns and 2 title patterns---Reuters datelines, photo credits, captions, bylines, \texttt{[VIDEO]} tags, outlet suffixes, URL debris---while keeping content words such as ``told Reuters''; (ii) drop empty and very short texts; (iii) de-duplicate greedily, first exact SHA-1 matches and then MinHash near-duplicates (Jaccard $\geq 0.9$); and (iv) split 80/10/10, stratified, seed 42, giving 48{,}639 / 6{,}080 / 6{,}080 WELFake and 30{,}053 / 3{,}757 / 3{,}757 ISOT rows. Splits are stored as id lists and every model reads the same ids.

\textbf{Leakage audit: ISOT $\subset$ WELFake.} WELFake was assembled from four corpora, one of which is ISOT. Hashing normalised text shows that 37{,}542 of the 37{,}567 processed ISOT articles (99.93\,\%) occur verbatim in processed WELFake, so ``train on WELFake, test on ISOT'' is an in-domain experiment. For the cross-domain experiment we therefore use \textbf{WELFake$\setminus$ISOT}: the WELFake rows not hash-matched to ISOT, minus 25 MinHash near-duplicates, i.e.\ 23{,}232 rows. A second audit trains a logistic regression on the 26 artefact-pattern counts alone (no words): it reaches 0.9973 five-fold accuracy on raw ISOT but 0.5568 on processed ISOT, which confirms that raw ISOT can be separated by source boilerplate alone and that our stripping removes that shortcut.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
%% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Claim Identification}
The input is cleaned and sentence-split with spaCy \texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm}. Sentences of fewer than six tokens, questions and imperatives are dropped; each remaining sentence $s$ at position $i$ receives the check-worthiness score
\begin{multline}
h(s) = 1.0\,[\mathrm{NE}] + 0.8\,[\mathrm{NUM}] + 0.6\,[\mathrm{REP}] + 0.5\,[i<3] \\
 + 0.4\,[\mathrm{CMP}] - 0.8\,[\mathrm{OPN}] - 0.5\,[\mathrm{HDG}],
\end{multline}
where the indicators denote a PERSON/ORG/GPE/EVENT/NORP/LOC entity, a CARDINAL/PERCENT/MONEY/DATE/QUANTITY entity, a reporting verb (\emph{said, announced, according to}, \dots), a lead sentence, a number or comparative, a first-person opinion cue (\emph{I think, should, must}) and a hedge (\emph{may, might, could}). The score is normalised to $[0,1]$ by its maximum (3.3) and clipped. Near-duplicate sentences (MiniLM cosine $>0.85$) are removed and the top $k=5$ are kept; a headline is always claim~1 with score 1. The optional ClaimBuster-trained classifier~\cite{arslan2020claimbuster} is not used yet.

\subsection{Evidence Retrieval}
\emph{Online}, each claim is sent to DuckDuckGo text search (\texttt{ddgs}), the Wikipedia search API (top page intros) and, when an API key is configured, the Google Fact Check Tools \texttt{claims:search} endpoint, each with a 6\,s timeout. Result pages are fetched and extracted with trafilatura, dated with htmldate, and reduced to the three sentences most similar to the claim. Result pages of Tier-1 fact-checkers are also parsed for their published rating (schema.org ClaimReview, or PolitiFact's Truth-O-Meter), so that a fact-check found by web search carries its rating like an index item. All network answers are cached in SQLite so that runs are repeatable. \emph{Offline}, the claim is embedded with \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}~\cite{reimers2019sbert,wang2020minilm} and searched in a FAISS~\cite{johnson2021faiss} \texttt{IndexFlatIP} over L2-normalised vectors (cosine) of all 12{,}836 LIAR statements and 1{,}056 FakeNewsNet titles (13{,}892 vectors, 20.35\,MB); hits with cosine $<0.55$ are discarded. Each item receives a source-reliability weight $w_s$ from an editable domain list: fact-checkers 1.0, wire services and official sites 0.8, Wikipedia 0.6, unknown domains 0.3, satire/hoax sites 0 (flagged).

\subsection{Stance Detection}
Each (claim, evidence) pair is scored by the cross-encoder \texttt{cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-small}, a DeBERTaV3~\cite{he2023debertav3} model trained on SNLI~\cite{bowman2015snli} and MultiNLI~\cite{williams2018mnli} only; we do not fine-tune it. With premise = evidence and hypothesis = claim, entailment, contradiction and neutral map to \textsc{Supports}, \textsc{Refutes} and \textsc{Neutral} with probability $p$. For items that carry a fact-checker rating (LIAR, FakeNewsNet, Google Fact Check, parsed ClaimReview) the rating is turned into a stance with $p=0.95$, but only when the claim restates the rated statement (NLI entailment, or cosine $\geq 0.75$ without contradiction). The signed contribution of an item is $\sigma(e)=\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{stance})\cdot p\cdot w_s$.

\subsection{Temporal and Context Check}
Three flags are computed for the main claim. \emph{Old news}: the input contains a recency cue (\emph{breaking, just in, today, \dots}) and the gap between the input date $t_\mathrm{post}$ and the (lower) median date of the supporting evidence is at least $N=6$ months; or the claim names its own date and that date lies at least $N$ months after the supporting evidence. Evidence without a date is not used. \emph{Out of context}: the claim's place (GPE) or year entities conflict with those of at least two supporting passages. \emph{Headline--body mismatch}: NLI with the body as premise refutes the headline with $p\geq 0.7$.

\subsection{Verdict Fusion and Confidence}
For claim $i$ let $S_i=\sum_e \sigma(e)$ and let $E_i$ be the number of non-neutral items with $w_s\geq 0.5$. The main stance is \textsc{Supports} if $S_1\geq\tau$, \textsc{Refutes} if $S_1\leq-\tau$ and \textsc{Neutral} otherwise, with $\tau=0.8$. Table~\ref{tab:rules} lists the decision rules, evaluated top to bottom (first match wins). The thresholds are those of \texttt{src/fusion/fusion.yaml}: $\tau=0.8$, minimum evidence $m=3$, $N=6$, $k=5$. They were selected by a grid search over $\tau$, $m$ and $N$ on the 30 dev claims and the 15 temporal-set controls only, with the selection rule fixed in advance; this moved $\tau$ from 0.6 to 0.8 and $m$ from 2 to 3 and raised dev macro-F1 from 0.0857 to 0.1165, i.e.\ by one claim, so it is a sanity check rather than a precise optimum. The content classifier's fake probability $p_\mathrm{fake}$ appears in only two places: the R8 tie-break and a $\pm 0.10$ confidence adjustment.

The confidence combines how one-sided, how broad and how reliable the evidence for the main claim is:
\begin{align}
a &= |S_1| \,/\, \textstyle\left(\sum_e p_e w_e + 10^{-6}\right), \quad c = \min(E_1,4)/4,\\
q &= \mathrm{mean}(w_s \text{ over non-neutral items}),\\
\mathrm{conf} &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(0.5a + 0.3c + 0.2q + \delta,\; 0.05,\; 0.98\right),
\end{align}
where $\delta=+0.10$ if the classifier agrees with a \cls{Fake}/\cls{Real} verdict ($p_\mathrm{fake}\geq 0.7$ / $\leq 0.3$), $-0.10$ if it disagrees and 0 otherwise. For \cls{Unverifiable}, $\mathrm{conf}=1-\max(a,0.2)$, i.e.\ how sure the system is that it cannot tell. R0 and the language check use fixed confidences of 0.9 and 0.8. Every response returns the rule that fired, the claims, the evidence cards with source, date, cosine and stance, and the temporal flags.

\subsection{Content Classifiers}
We train TF-IDF word $n$-gram models with multinomial Naive Bayes, logistic regression and a linear SVM (scikit-learn), and fine-tune \texttt{distilbert-base-uncased}~\cite{sanh2019distilbert,wolf2020transformers} on \texttt{title [SEP] text}. A second DistilBERT head is fine-tuned on LIAR statements (text only, no speaker or party, which are label proxies) for TRUE / MIXED / FALSE with class-balanced cross-entropy. The deployed style signal is the full-data DistilBERT checkpoint. WELFake and ISOT use a stratified 80/10/10 split (seed 42) and LIAR its official split; hyper-parameters are chosen by validation macro-F1 (5-fold grid search for the classical models), and all training ran on one laptop GPU (RTX 2050, 4\,GB).

\textbf{A leakage finding.} Our first end-to-end run (v1) scored 0.2308 accuracy and 0.1854 macro-F1. Inspecting its evidence showed that web search often returned the claim's \emph{own} fact-check page---the very page the gold label was taken from---and that for 34 of the 65 claims this page was among the kept evidence (a lower bound, matched on normalised URLs). Removing those items after the fact and re-fusing lowers v1 to 0.1538 accuracy and 0.1260 macro-F1, the honest v1 baseline. We therefore added an evaluation-only guard that drops evidence whose normalised or canonical URL equals the claim's fact-check URL; in v2 it removed the own page for 35 of 65 claims, while 22 claims kept at least one \emph{other} matching fact-check, which is legitimate evidence. In deployment, finding an existing fact-check is the intended behaviour; in evaluation it silently turns the task into label lookup. Any evaluation of retrieval-based verification on claims taken from fact-checkers needs such a guard.

\subsection{Temporal Test Set}
We built a separate set of 30 verified fact-checks: 15 items of old material recirculated as new and 15 current controls. Run online, the old-news flag \textbf{never fired}: 0 true positives, 0 false positives, 15 false negatives and 15 true negatives, i.e.\ 50\,\% correct, so objective O5 ($\geq 80$\,\%) is \textbf{not met}. Of the 15 missed items, 7 had no dated supporting evidence, 3 had dated evidence but neither a recency cue nor a date in the claim, and 5 had both but a gap below $N=6$ months, because the supporting pages were recent re-reports or fact-checks of the old material. Fact-check phrasings rarely contain ``breaking'' or ``today'', and the dated evidence the check needs is precisely what retrieval of a recirculated story does not return.

\subsection{Latency and Resources}
On the laptop CPU (8 threads; other jobs running, so the numbers are pessimistic) the median end-to-end time per claim is 40\,ms offline (p90 164\,ms), 2{,}890\,ms online with a warm cache (p90 4{,}611\,ms) and 11{,}032\,ms online when pages must be fetched (p90 15{,}869\,ms; median retrieval stage 9{,}683\,ms, measured on the first evaluation pass); the median stance stage takes 1{,}154\,ms online with a warm cache. With all models loaded (MiniLM, the NLI cross-encoder, spaCy and DistilBERT) the process peaks at 2{,}373.8\,MB resident memory, within our 4\,GB CPU budget. The system ships as a web interface (analyse, result and about screens with evidence cards, a dates panel, LIME highlights and JSON download) served from one CPU-only Docker image of about 3.1\,GB with all models baked in. Run offline with networking disabled, the container answered all five gallery examples with their intended verdicts at a median of 0.50\,s (0.22\,s on repeat) and a peak resident memory of 1.33\,GB.

\subsection{Case Studies and Failure Analysis}
Real outputs illustrate both the strengths and the failure modes. \emph{``WHO declared that 5G towers spread COVID-19.''} is labelled \cls{Fake} by R3: four reliable sources refute it (five refuting, five neutral passages). \emph{``Michelle Obama is a man.''} (LC020) is \cls{Fake} by R3 with confidence 0.98. The true headline \emph{``ISRO's Chandrayaan-3 landed near the lunar south pole on 23 Aug 2023''} ends in \cls{Unverifiable} (R9, one supporting, three refuting, six neutral passages) while DistilBERT gives it $P(\text{fake})=0.9797$; a classifier-first system would have called a true headline fake. LC003, a true claim that U.S.\ families paid more than \$1{,}700 each in tariff costs, ends in \cls{Unverifiable} (R1) although a Joint Economic Committee passage restating it was retrieved (cosine 0.85): the NLI model labelled all ten passages neutral. The hyper-local \emph{``A local shop in Ghaziabad sold 500 phones in one hour''} is correctly \cls{Unverifiable} in v2 but was \cls{Fake} by R3 in v1: two reliable passages about a phone theft in Ghaziabad were read as refuting---the not-enough-information-to-\textsc{Refutes} confusion measured on FEVER---and only the stricter $m=3$ rule now stops them. Sixteen malformed or adversarial inputs (empty, emoji-only, Hindi, 60k-character, HTML injection, URL in offline mode, \dots) produced no server error.

\textbf{Where existing systems beat us.} Manual fact-checkers are far more accurate: our labels come from them, and the system matches their rating on only 13 of the 65 held-out claims (accuracy 0.2000). Binary style classifiers report higher accuracy on their own benchmarks---and so does our DistilBERT at 0.9898 macro-F1---but those numbers measure the style of one corpus; across disjoint corpora our classifiers lose 0.29--0.44 macro-F1 and the DistilBERT models of~\cite{blackledge2021transforming} fall from 0.989 to 0.670 accuracy. FEVER verifiers such as KGAT are much stronger on Wikipedia claims, where retrieval targets a closed, well-formed corpus; our untuned stance model alone misses 0.85 even with gold evidence. When a matching fact-check exists, a fact-check search tool returns a professional rating, which is more reliable than our NLI over news passages.

\textbf{What we do that they cannot.} (i) \emph{A verdict when no fact-check exists}: R3 and R7 decide from ordinary news and reference sources (e.g.\ the 5G case), which a fact-check search tool cannot. (ii) \emph{A date check}: no system in Table~\ref{tab:compare} compares the dates of a claim and its evidence; ours can turn a supported but outdated claim into \cls{Misleading}, although on our temporal set it has not yet fired. (iii) \emph{Honest abstention}: \cls{Unverifiable} is a first-class answer with an uncertainty sentence, and every verdict names the rule and the evidence behind it. Our abstention is currently too frequent (precision 0.0943), but when the system does commit to \cls{Real} or \cls{Fake} its precision is 0.6667 for each.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Low end-to-end performance.} Five-class macro-F1 is 0.1667 against a target of 0.55; 53 of 65 answers are \cls{Unverifiable} and \cls{Partially True} and \cls{Misleading} are never predicted.
  \item \emph{Small evaluation set, single labelling.} 65 held-out claims (95 in all) with labels mapped from one fact-checker's rating; no second annotator or agreement statistic, and the fusion thresholds were tuned on only 30 claims.
  \item \emph{Retrieval dependence.} Results depend on an unofficial DuckDuckGo interface and on what the web returns on the day; no Google Fact Check key was used, and the offline index covers only 36.9\,\% of recent claims.
  \item \emph{Zero-shot stance.} The NLI model reaches 0.7267 on FEVER, confuses not-enough-information with \textsc{Refutes}, and labelled a near-verbatim restatement neutral (LC003).
  \item \emph{Temporal check unproven.} It fired on none of the 15 recirculated items, because the dated supporting evidence it needs is rarely retrieved.
  \item \emph{Scope.} English and text only; 7 of the 65 held-out claims concern an image or a video.
  \item \emph{Classifier evidence incomplete.} DistilBERT was not trained at length 512 nor evaluated cross-domain; the source-reliability tiers are hand-made; and the leakage guard matches URLs only, so it is a lower bound on what it removes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ===========================================================================
We presented an evidence-grounded, explainable pipeline that verifies a headline, article or URL into one of five verdicts---\cls{Real}, \cls{Fake}, \cls{Partially True}, \cls{Misleading} or \cls{Unverifiable}---using only free tools and a student laptop. Claims are extracted, matched against free online sources and an offline FAISS index of fact-checked statements, labelled for stance by an NLI cross-encoder, checked for temporal and contextual mismatch, and fused by a transparent decision table that reports the rule that fired. The content classifier may only adjust confidence or break a single-source tie.

The style classifiers are strong in-domain---DistilBERT reaches 0.9898 test macro-F1 on WELFake, the tuned SVM 0.9481---but this does not transfer: trained on WELFake$\setminus$ISOT and tested on ISOT the SVM falls to 0.6611, in the reverse direction to 0.5480, and an artefact-only classifier separates raw ISOT at 0.9973. On 65 recent held-out fact-checked claims the full system reaches 0.2000 accuracy and 0.1667 five-class macro-F1 online and 0.0343 offline, well below our target; the zero-shot stance model reaches 0.7267 on FEVER and the old-news check did not fire on our temporal set. We also found that our own first evaluation leaked the gold fact-check page into the evidence for 34 of 65 claims and over-stated macro-F1 (0.1854 instead of 0.1260); we report the guarded numbers and recommend such a guard for any evaluation that draws claims from fact-checkers.

Future work follows from these results: fine-tune the stance model on not-enough-information pairs, query the Google Fact Check Tools API, use the claim's own year and dated evidence in the temporal check, learn the fusion thresholds from a larger, double-labelled claim set, complete the length-512 and cross-domain DistilBERT runs, and add Hindi/Hinglish input and OCR for image-based claims.
